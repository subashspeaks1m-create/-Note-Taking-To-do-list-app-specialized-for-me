# Psychology of Website

A website that makes money is a salesperson that never sleeps. Visitors arrive with a problem and a head full of doubts. The page sells when each section answers the next doubt *before* the visitor finishes thinking it. Nothing else matters as much: not 3D models, not scroll-triggered video, not motion graphics. Those can make a page memorable, but copy and structure are what make it sell.

This skill turns that idea into a repeatable method you can use for any business: a DTC product, a local plumber, a SaaS tool, an agency, a coach.

## The core model: answer the internal monologue

Every visitor scrolls with a running inner voice. Your job is to write the page as the other half of that conversation. In rough order, the voice asks:

| # | The visitor thinks | The page answers with |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Is this for me?" | A hero line that names their situation or the outcome they want |
| 2 | "What is it, and what does it do?" | One plain sentence: what you sell, how it helps |
| 3 | "Does it fix *my* problem?" | Their problem in their own words, then how the product solves it |
| 4 | "Does it actually work? Who else uses it?" | Proof: reviews, numbers, video testimonials, press, experts |
| 5 | "Why is it so expensive?" | Price justification: quality, process, ingredients, standards, what it replaces |
| 6 | "Why you and not the cheaper option?" | Us vs them, the specific difference |
| 7 | "What if it doesn't work for me?" | More proof, a guarantee, FAQs that handle objections |
| 8 | "Okay. What do I do now?" | One clear call to action, with no detours |

Most underperforming sites fail because they skip questions (no proof, no price justification) or answer them out of order (a features grid before the visitor even knows it is for them). Use this table as your diagnostic lens for everything below.

**Why it works.** People rarely buy because of a single point. They buy when doubts run out. Each section that answers a question and repeats a reason to believe ("81,000 five-star reviews", "formulated by nutritional scientists", "3.3 million customers") adds to the visitor's confidence. Get a visitor past the halfway point of the page with every doubt answered, and they are very likely to buy, even if they end up buying somewhere else later.

## Principles that drive every decision

1. **Sell to one person, not everyone.** "I'd rather have a 100% chance at $100 than a 50% chance at $1,000." A page written for "everyone" speaks to no one. A page written for "a woman in her 40s whose hormones changed and who misses feeling like herself" makes the right visitor feel understood instantly, and nearly everyone who clicks an ad or searches a niche term is that person or close to them (they're buying for a mother, a sister, a partner). Pick the ideal customer profile (ICP) and write only to them.

2. **Read it as the buyer, never as the designer.** Before you judge or write any section, become the ICP: their day, their frustration, what they have already tried, what they are afraid of wasting money on. Copy written from inside the buyer's head beats copy written about the product. See `references/icp-research.md`.

3. **One page, one story.** Choose a single narrative angle (an enemy, a belief, a transformation) and make every section reinforce it. Example: a cold-pressed juice brand built entirely around *sugar*: "Stop drinking sugar. Try the real thing." Then "Sugar should come from the fruit, not the packet." A single story is memorable. Ten scattered benefits are not.

4. **Proof is repeated, not mentioned once.** Put proof right after the hero, after the problem section, after the price justification, and right before the final CTA. Vary the type each time (numbers, video, text reviews, press logos, experts, certifications). Don't overdo it, though: wall after wall of identical praise starts to feel fake.

5. **You are not Dior.** Dior, Apple, Shopify, and Amazon can run minimal, vague, mood-only sites because their brand already did the selling before anyone landed. A small or growing business has no such advantage, so its copy has to do the work. When a client asks for "a site like Dior", take the *aesthetic cues* (whitespace, typography, motion) and keep the *conversion structure*. See `references/client-conversations.md` for how to say this.

6. **Design serves clarity.** Visual effects are welcome when they carry the story and stay fast and readable on phones. They are never a substitute for the questions in the table above. If you are pairing this skill with a cinematic or scroll-driven build skill, this skill decides *what the page says and in what order*. The other skill decides *how it looks and moves*.

7. **The website exists to return money.** If a client pays 100 and gets 100 back, it was a waste of their time. If they get 150 back, you're a magician they'll keep paying. Every recommendation should tie back to more leads, more sales, or a higher order value. This is also what justifies premium pricing (see `references/client-conversations.md`).

8. **One primary action.** Every section leads toward the same CTA ("Buy Raw", "Book a free inspection", "Start free trial"). Don't send visitors off into a quiz, a blog, or a menu of five equal buttons when a direct CTA would convert. Use a quiz only when the product genuinely needs a recommendation step, and even then, offer a direct buy path next to it.

## The page structure: KISS (Keep It Super Simple)

This is the default skeleton. It is a strong starting point, not a cage: merge, repeat, or reorder sections when the business calls for it, and say why when you do.

1. **Hero.** One line that answers at least one of: *What do you do? How does it work? What do you sell? How does the customer benefit?* Add a short subline, a proof snippet (star rating, customer count), and the primary CTA. Example: "Get your old self back. Naturally." ★★★★★ 81,756 reviews.
2. **Problem + how the product helps** (usually two consecutive sections). Name the pain in the ICP's words, then show the product as the fix. Pattern: *"Going through X? Choose Y and get Z."* Example: "Hit snooze on sleepless nights. Maximize rest for optimal recovery."
3. **Testimonial section, video-first.** Real people describing a real before and after. Video builds trust fastest. Use text or photos if video isn't available.
4. **Price justification + why we're better.** Quality standards, sourcing, process, credentials, what it replaces, us vs them comparison. This answers "why so expensive?" before it becomes a reason to leave.
5. **Second testimonial section, image or text based.** A different format and different people from section 3. Add expert or press endorsements if available.
6. **Final CTA.** Restate the outcome, remove the last risk (guarantee, free shipping, no contract), and make one clear action. Example: "Ready for better sleep? Try Knock."

Optional sections that slot in when they answer a real question: a trust bar ("as featured in", key numbers), a product range or offers grid, how it works in 3 steps, FAQ and objection handling, guarantee, founder story, and repeated CTAs between sections.

For section-by-section detail, including what to write, the visual direction, common mistakes, and variants for products, local services, SaaS, and B2B agencies, read `references/page-framework.md`.

## Workflows

Figure out which of these the user needs. Often it's more than one.

### A. Plan and write a new website (or the copy for one)

1. **Gather the essentials.** Collect the offer, price, who it's for, the CTA destination (checkout, booking, form, call), what proof actually exists (reviews, numbers, press, credentials, guarantees, case studies), competitors, and any brand voice. Ask only for what you can't infer. If the user wants you to keep moving, make sensible assumptions and label them as assumptions in the output.
2. **Become the ICP.** Run the immersion exercise in `references/icp-research.md`: a vivid day in their life, pains, desires, objections, and the exact words they use. If web search is available, mine real reviews, Reddit threads, and forums for voice-of-customer language. Real buyer phrases make the best headlines.
3. **Build the internal question map.** List the questions this specific ICP will have, in the order they'll arise, and assign each to the section that answers it. This map is the skeleton of the page and the thing you check the page against at the end.
4. **Choose the single narrative angle.** Use one enemy or one transformation. Offer the user two or three angles with a one-line rationale each if the right one isn't obvious.
5. **Write the copy deck** using `assets/copy-deck-template.md`. Write it section by section, noting the question each section answers, the headline, subline, body, proof element, CTA, and visual direction. Follow `references/copywriting.md`.
6. **Run the scroll test.** Re-read the deck top to bottom *as the ICP*, voicing the inner monologue out loud. Wherever a doubt goes unanswered, or a section answers a question nobody has asked yet, fix it. Then run the checklist at the end of this file.
7. **If you're also building the site,** lock the copy first, then design around it. Keep the CTA visible and repeated, make it fast, and check it on a phone first, since most ad traffic is mobile.

### B. Audit or redesign an existing website

1. **Get the page.** Run `python scripts/page_outline.py <url-or-file>` to pull the heading order, CTAs, proof signals, and quick flags. Pages built with JavaScript may come back nearly empty. If so, render the page in a browser (Playwright, if available), or ask the user for screenshots or pasted copy.
2. **Do the monologue walkthrough.** Scroll the page as the ICP and narrate the inner voice section by section ("Okay, this is for me... wow, 3.3 million customers... hmm, $90 is a lot... oh, the world's highest quality standards, that makes sense"). This narration is the most persuasive part of an audit, for clients and for you.
3. **Score it** with the rubric in `references/audit-rubric.md` and list the gaps in order of conversion impact.
4. **Rewrite.** Produce a redesigned section map and the new copy deck, as in workflow A, steps 3 to 6. A before/after per section makes the improvement obvious.

### C. Find a target audience the client can't describe

New businesses often can't name their ICP. Use the immersion prompt and the questioning in `references/icp-research.md` to propose two or three candidate ICPs, then recommend one with reasoning.

### D. Pitch, price, or handle a client

When the user is selling the website itself (pricing a project, overcoming "make it look like Dior", fighting imposter syndrome about charging $3.5k to $10k), use `references/client-conversations.md`.

## Social proof: powerful, and it must be real

Testimonials are close to non-negotiable. A page without them rarely converts. But **never invent customers, reviews, ratings, or testimonial videos and present them as real.** Fake reviews are illegal in many markets. The US FTC's rule on fake reviews and testimonials, in force since October 2024, explicitly covers AI-generated ones, and the UK's DMCC Act bans them too. They also destroy trust and the client's brand when someone notices, and someone usually does. When a client has no proof yet, use the honest playbook in `references/social-proof.md`. It covers incentivized reviews from real past buyers (disclosed), beta users, case studies, founder credibility, guarantees, process transparency, certifications, and press.

For mockups and pitch drafts, it's fine to include clearly labeled placeholder testimonials, like `[PLACEHOLDER: replace with a real customer review before launch]`, so the layout can be judged. Flag them in your handoff. AI-generated imagery is fine for product shots, lifestyle scenes, and illustrations. It's not fine for faking customers.

## Copy essentials (quick version)

Read `references/copywriting.md` for formulas and worked examples. The short version:

- Write in the buyer's words, not the brand's. "Hot flashes gone in 4 weeks" beats "supports hormonal wellness."
- Be specific. Use numbers, names, timeframes, and concrete results.
- Lead with the outcome, then the mechanism. Features come last and only as reasons to believe.
- Keep headlines short and readable in one glance, with sublines that do the explaining.
- Cut generic AI-sounding filler, such as "unlock", "elevate", "seamless", "revolutionize", "in today's fast-paced world", "take it to the next level". Generic copy can't hit the spots that make a specific person feel understood. Earn every line by writing from inside the ICP's head.
- Make every CTA say what happens next ("Book my free quote", not "Submit").

## Final checklist

Before calling any page done, confirm:

- [ ] The hero tells a stranger in five seconds what this is, who it's for, and why they should care.
- [ ] One ICP and one narrative angle run through every section.
- [ ] Every question in the internal-monologue table is answered, in a sensible order.
- [ ] The problem is described in the buyer's own language.
- [ ] Proof shows up at least three times, in at least two formats, and all of it is real (or clearly labeled as a placeholder).
- [ ] The price is justified before the visitor can object to it.
- [ ] There's a clear "why us, not them".
- [ ] One primary CTA, repeated, with specific action wording and risk reversal near the final one.
- [ ] No section exists only to look cool. Each answers a question or moves the visitor forward.
- [ ] It reads well and loads fast on a phone.
- [ ] Assumptions, placeholders, and anything the client must supply are listed in the handoff.
