# Client Conversations: Pricing, Positioning, and Pushback

Many web designers undercharge because, deep down, they don't believe the site will earn the client back what they paid. The fix isn't confidence tricks. It's knowing that your process (ICP research, an internal question map, conversion structure, real proof) produces a site that sells. Once you understand why people buy, charging $3,500 to $10,000 for a website becomes an honest offer, not a bluff.

## Frame every project as a return on investment

- A client who pays 100 and gets 100 back has wasted their time. A client who pays 100 and gets 150 back thinks you're a magician and keeps paying you. Aim for the site to return many times its price.
- Talk in their numbers: "If your average order is $60 and this page converts 1% better on 10,000 monthly visitors, that's 100 extra orders, or $6,000 a month."
- Sell the outcome (leads, sales, booked calls) and the process that gets there, not "a modern, responsive design".

## Why this is worth $3.5k–$10k (what to show in a proposal)

1. **ICP research and voice-of-customer mining:** we learn exactly who buys and why, in their words.
2. **An internal question map and narrative angle:** a page built to answer every doubt in order.
3. **Conversion copy, section by section**, with the reasoning for each.
4. **A proof strategy:** collecting real reviews, case studies, and guarantees, and placing them.
5. **Design and build**, mobile-first and fast, with one clear CTA path.
6. **A before-and-after audit** showing the gaps in the current site (use `audit-rubric.md`). The monologue walkthrough of their current site is often what closes the deal.

## "I want my site to look like Dior / Apple / Shopify / Amazon"

Respect the taste, and explain the difference maturely:

> "Dior's site can be almost all mood because everyone already wants a Dior perfume. Their brand did the selling years before anyone visits. We don't have that advantage yet, so your site has to do the selling: tell people exactly what you do, show proof, and answer their doubts. We can absolutely borrow the premium look (the whitespace, the typography, the motion), but we'll keep the structure that turns visitors into buyers. Once you're as big as Dior, we can strip it back."

Find the middle ground: a premium aesthetic and a conversion structure.

## "Can we just make it look cool?" (3D, scroll-video, motion)

Effects are fine as seasoning. A scroll-driven video can make a hero unforgettable. But they don't sell by themselves, and they can slow the page down or hide the message on phones. Agree to use effects that support the story, and keep the copy and structure as the backbone.

## "We don't have testimonials."

See `social-proof.md`. Offer to run a quick real-review drive (incentivized and disclosed), a small beta, or case studies, and use guarantees and founder credibility in the meantime. Don't offer to fake them. Explain that fake reviews are illegal in the US, the UK, and elsewhere, and that they put the client's brand at risk.

## "Our product is for everyone."

Walk them through the narrowing exercise in `icp-research.md`, section 4. Explain the 100% of $100 vs 50% of $1,000 idea: a focused page converts the core buyer far better, and adjacent buyers still recognize themselves.

## Discovery questions for the first call

1. Who has bought from you so far, and why did they choose you?
2. What do customers say right before they buy? What almost stops them?
3. Where does your traffic come from (ads, search, referrals, social)?
4. What happens after someone clicks the main button (checkout, call, form)?
5. What proof do you have (reviews anywhere, numbers, press, credentials, guarantees)?
6. Who are your main competitors, and why do people pick them instead?
7. What's an average order or client worth to you? (This is your ROI math.)
8. What would make this project a clear win in 90 days?
